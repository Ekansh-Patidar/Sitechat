1. The one-sentence version, and the real purpose

Sitechat is a chat assistant that a business adds to its website with one line of HTML; it answers visitors' questions from that business's own documents and facts, and passes interested visitors to the business's team by email.

That sentence hides a decision that changed the design halfway through. At first the project was thought of as customer support: answer questions so fewer people email the school. Partway through, it was reframed as lead capture. The project record says it plainly: "Answering questions is how the bot earns the right to ask for contact details. Every conversation should end with the business knowing who was asking and how to reach them."

Why that reframing matters so much: a support bot is judged by "did it answer?". A lead-capture bot is judged by "did the school end up with a person to talk to?". Many features only make sense under the second goal:

  • Every visitor question is recorded on the server, so the school's email lists all of them (Phase 4b).
  • A "Talk to the team" button is always visible after the first reply (Phase 5).
  • A visitor who gave their email but never asked to be contacted still produces an email 15 to 20 minutes after they go quiet (the "lead digest", Phase 4b).
  • Questions the bot could not answer are labelled in the email, so the school knows what to reply to first.

The first customers in mind: yoga teacher training schools in India, selling 28-day residential courses to international students. This is a "high-consideration purchase": someone reads for weeks before paying over a thousand dollars and flying to Rishikesh. The bot's job is to remove doubt ("Is the food vegetarian?", "Is this too advanced for me?") and then hand a warm, informed visitor to a human.

Analogy

A good receptionist at a language school. They know the brochure by heart and answer the easy questions on the spot. When a question needs the head teacher, they don't guess: they write down the visitor's name, email and every question they asked, and put the note on the head teacher's desk. Their real value isn't the answers; it's that nobody who walked in interested leaves without the school knowing about them.

Say it in an interview

"It's a multi-tenant RAG chatbot, but the product goal is lead capture, not deflection. That goal drove the architecture: the server records every question deterministically instead of trusting the model to remember, and a scheduled job emails the business about any visitor who left contact details, even if they never explicitly asked to be contacted."

2. The three kinds of people

Three different people touch Sitechat, and they never see the same screens:

VisitorA prospective student

Browses the school's website, opens the chat bubble, asks questions, maybe types their email. Sees only the widget. Never logs in.

Tenant (customer)The school and its team

Pastes the script tag on their site once, keeps a Google Sheet of prices and dates up to date, and receives lead emails in their normal inbox. Never sees our code or a dashboard (none exists yet).

OperatorYou

Creates the school's account, prepares and loads its documents, runs the evaluation, deploys, watches logs and costs. Works through Python scripts in scripts/ run from a terminal.

The word tenant is used everywhere in the code. It comes from buildings: one apartment block (our server), many tenants (schools), each with their own locked flat (their data). Section 9 explains how the locks work.

In Sitechat

The one tenant configured today is pyramid-yogshala (display name "Pyramid Yogshala", site key pk_pyramid_yogshala_001). It used to be called demo-yoga; the rename is one of the challenges in section 14. The "team email" on it is your own address, because there is no real customer yet.

3. A visitor's journey, step by step

Here is everything a visitor experiences, with what happens behind each step. These are real screenshots of the real widget.js, running against the real app on this laptop with in-memory storage and a scripted stand-in for the AI (so no money was spent and no real visitor data is shown).

  1. The page loads. The school's page contains <script src=".../widget/widget.js" data-site-key="pk_...">. The browser downloads our script from our server. The script draws a round "Chat with us" button in the corner, inside a shadow root so the school's CSS can't break it (Phase 5).
  2. The widget asks who it is. It calls GET /config with the site key. The server looks up the tenant, checks the page's domain is on that tenant's list, and returns only public settings: name, greeting, suggested questions, colour, privacy link. If the key or domain is wrong, the visitor sees "not available" and the site owner sees the exact reason in the browser console.
  3. The launcher teases. After 3 seconds, the button widens to show a short suggested question ("What food is provided?"), cycling through up to three, then rests 30 seconds. This was a deliberate experiment to invite clicks, and visitors who ask for reduced motion never see it.
  4. The visitor asks. The widget sends POST /chat with {"message": ..., "conversation_id": ...}. The first time, there is no conversation id; the server makes one and the widget saves it in the browser's localStorage for 30 days, so a returning visitor continues the same conversation.
  5. The server prepares an answer. In order: check the daily AI budget; remove any email or phone number from the message and store it separately; work out which course the visitor means; fetch the conversation so far and the most relevant passages from the school's documents (at the same time); add the school's verified facts; then ask the AI model. Phase 4b goes through every step.
  6. The reply comes back within a few seconds. The widget splits it at blank lines into bubbles and shows them one after another, with typing dots in between, "like someone texting". Bold text is rendered, but nothing from the server is ever inserted as HTML (that would let the AI's text become code running on the school's page).
  7. After the reply is sent, the server, in the background, stores the turn, records the visitor's question, summarises older messages if the conversation is long, and decides whether the team should be emailed now.
  8. The visitor asks for a person (by saying so, accepting the bot's offer, or clicking "Talk to the team"). The AI calls a tool, contact_team. The server records a "contact request". If there's no email yet, the bot is told to ask for one.
  9. The visitor gives an email. The server pulls it out of the message before the AI sees it, replaces it with [email provided], and stores it on the visitor's lead. The widget shows "Your email goes only to Pyramid Yogshala's team" the first time. The server's response carries a confirmation and the widget shows it as a card. The lead email goes out in the background.
  10. The visitor leaves. If they gave an email but some questions haven't been emailed yet, a job that runs every 5 minutes emails them once the visitor has been quiet for 15 minutes.
Analogy

The visitor sees a calm conversation. Behind the counter, three things happen that they never see: a clerk tears off the slip with their email and files it separately (so it isn't passed around), a researcher pulls the right pages from the school's binder before each answer, and after they walk away, a secretary types up a summary for the head teacher.

4. The school team's side: the lead email

The school never logs in anywhere. Everything they get from Sitechat arrives as an email in their normal inbox. This is the real email produced by the conversation in the screenshots above (rendered from the real code; the summary and labels were scripted for this capture).

What each part is for, and the reason behind it:

PartWhy it is there
Green note"They asked the team to get in touch. The chat told them you usually reply within 24 hours." The team needs to know what the visitor was promised, so the promise is repeated from the tenant's reply_time setting.
ContactEmail, phone, name, country, when they plan to come, what they're interested in: whatever was captured. A throwaway address (Mailinator and similar) is flagged "may not be real".
What they askedEvery question the visitor asked since the last email, each labelled Not answered, Partly answered, Answered, Comment or Not checked. The server records the questions itself; an AI call only tidies the wording and adds the labels. If the AI drops one, the server lists it anyway as "Not checked" (you can see that in the capture: the AI's reply covered two of three).
SummaryThree or four sentences, always in English (if the visitor wrote in Spanish, it ends "They wrote in Spanish.").
Suggested replyA template, not AI text: thanks, then each open question with "[your answer]" under it. Built by code on purpose: an AI draft would be tempted to fill in answers, and an invented answer sent under the school's name is the worst possible outcome.
Reply buttonOpens the team's own email program with the draft already in it. The email's Reply-To is the visitor, so pressing Reply in Gmail also reaches them.
Challenge we faced: the email that could be used as a weapon

Originally the visitor was copied (CC) on the lead email. A code review on 16 Oct found the problem: anyone can type anyone's address into a chat. A stranger could type a victim's email, ask a question containing a link, and leave; 15 minutes later the victim would receive an email from our sender containing text the stranger wrote. Scripted, that's a free spam and phishing relay, and it would get our email account blocked, which would stop lead emails for every school.

Fix (17 Oct): the email goes to the team only, with reply_to set to the visitor, so the team's reply still reaches them; links the visitor typed are shown unclickable (hxxp://evil[.]example); and a daily cap of 50 lead emails per school protects the free email quota of 100 a day. Lesson: any feature that sends something to an address a user typed is an abuse channel until proven otherwise.

5. The operator's side: onboarding a school

There is no sign-up page. Onboarding is a sequence of scripts you run from the project folder. Each one is a small Python program that talks to the real cloud services using the keys in your local .env file.

  1. Create the tenant. Edit the values in scripts/create_tenant.py (name, site key, allowed domains, greeting, suggested questions, team email, reply time, colour, the Google Sheet id) and run python -m scripts.create_tenant. It saves one document in Firestore's tenants collection.
  2. Prepare the documents. In docs/<tenant>/, write Markdown files: one "school-general" document for things true of every course (rooms, food, travel, teachers), and programme details per programme. Write a programmes.yaml listing each course, the names visitors use for it, and which document sections belong to it.
  3. Ingest. python -m scripts.ingest pyramid-yogshala docs/pyramid-yogshala splits each document into sections ("chunks"), turns each into a list of numbers that represents its meaning (an "embedding", Phase 3), and stores them in Pinecone. Today: 95 chunks.
  4. Load the facts. The school keeps prices, dates and deposits in a Google Sheet with three columns: Category, Label, Value. python -m scripts.sync_facts pyramid-yogshala reads it, validates every row, reports problems to fix, and logs exactly what changed. After that, the 5-minute job re-reads it once a day.
  5. Check retrieval quality. python -m scripts.evaluate pyramid-yogshala asks 82 labelled questions and measures how often the right sections are found. evaluate_answers checks the actual answers, and evaluate_attacks sends 29 misuse attempts.
  6. Install. Send the school the snippet and the install guide (notes/install-guide.md: WordPress, Wix, Squarespace, Shopify).
  7. Run it. Watch logs and Sentry; use scripts.unanswered to see what the bot couldn't answer (to improve the documents), scripts.feedback for thumbs-down replies, and scripts.leads to export every lead to a spreadsheet.
In Sitechat

Every script that deletes or rewrites data does a dry run by default and needs you to repeat the tenant's name to really do it: python -m scripts.delete_tenant pyramid-yogshala --delete --confirm pyramid-yogshala. A typo costs nothing; deleting the wrong school can't happen by accident.

Before Phase 4 opens each file, here is the map. Each box is a folder; arrows mean "calls".

One rule holds the design together: each layer only knows the layer below it. The API layer doesn't know what a prompt is. The leads package doesn't call the AI (the summary is written by the agent and handed over as text). The orchestrator doesn't know whether the data is in Firestore or in a Python dictionary. That is what lets 492 tests run in about a minute with no network: every outside service can be swapped for a stand-in (Phase 0, section 8.12).

Say it in an interview

"There's a thin HTTP layer, an orchestrator that owns everything about what a reply contains, and domain packages for retrieval, leads and storage behind abstract interfaces. The API layer has no AI knowledge, and the leads package has no dependency on the agent: we actually had a two-way dependency there and removed it. That separation is why the whole test suite runs offline with in-memory stores and a mocked model."

7. Two kinds of knowledge: facts and documents

Sitechat answers from two separate sources, and the split is one of the most important decisions in the project.

DocumentsFacts
WhatDescriptive text: curriculum, philosophy, daily schedule, what the rooms are like, how to get there.Exact, changeable details: fees, dates, deposits, inclusions.
Where it comes fromMarkdown files you prepare, in docs/<tenant>/.A Google Sheet the school edits itself (Category, Label, Value).
How the bot sees itOnly the few passages most relevant to the question are found and added (retrieval, Phase 3).All of them, in every conversation, labelled "Verified current details... authoritative. Use them exactly as written."
How it changesRe-run ingest.The school edits the sheet; it's re-read daily, or by hand with sync_facts.

Why split them? The trigger was something found on the school's real website: course dates listed as "December 2026" followed by "January 2026", months going backwards, because someone copied a block and didn't update the year. A bot answering from that page would tell a student about a course that had already happened. Prices and dates must come from one place the school controls and can see, not from paragraphs scattered across documents.

There is a second, technical reason. Retrieval finds passages by meaning, which is good for "what will I learn about the body?" but unreliable for "how much is the 300-hour course?": the 300-hour fees section and the 200-hour fees section look almost identical in meaning. Facts are small enough to send in full every time, so the bot never has to find a price; it always has all of them.

Analogy

The documents are the school's brochure, where you look up the right page when asked. The facts are the price list taped to the counter: always in view, updated by the office, never paraphrased.

In Sitechat

Considered and rejected: letting the school update facts by WhatsApp message. It would need Meta business verification, a webhook, a conversation state machine and per-message cost, "to solve a problem a spreadsheet solves in a day". A web dashboard is the eventual answer, and it would write to the same fact store, so nothing else would change.

8. What is stored, where, and for how long

Sitechat uses two databases. Firestore (Google's document database) holds everything except the embeddings; Pinecone (a vector database) holds the embeddings and a copy of each chunk's text. Phase 4e and Phase 6 go deeper.

CollectionOne document perPersonal data?Kept
tenantsschoolNo (the team's email)Until deleted
conversationsvisitor conversation: messages, running summary, every question, current course focus, digest flagsYes: what visitors wrote (contact details removed)365 days after last activity, then deleted automatically
leadsvisitor conversation: email, phone, name, country, contact requestsYes365 days after last activity
feedbackrated reply: thumbs up/down, reason (ids only)Points to it365 days
facts, programmesschoolNoUntil replaced
documents, chunksdocument / chunkNoUntil re-ingested
token_usage, lead_email_countsschool per dayNoKept (counters)

Three privacy decisions shape this table:

  • Contact details never reach the AI. They're cut out of the message and replaced with [email provided] before anything else happens, so they're not re-sent to OpenAI on every later turn or printed in logs of the conversation.
  • Leads are stored apart from conversations. Conversations are operational; leads are personal data. Separate collections can have separate access and retention without untangling them later.
  • Everything personal expires. Each write stamps an expire_at date a year ahead; a Firestore "TTL policy" deletes documents once the date passes. A visitor asking for deletion is handled by scripts.delete_tenant <tenant> --conversation <id>.

9. Multi-tenancy: many schools, one system

One server and one set of databases serve every school. The danger is obvious: school A's visitors must never see school B's documents, and a bug must not be able to cause that. Sitechat's rule, from the project record: "A filter you must remember to apply is a filter someone eventually forgets. A structural boundary cannot be forgotten."

DataHow it's kept separate
Conversations, leadsThe document id is tenant__conversation, e.g. pyramid-yogshala__3f2a.... Every store method takes tenant_id. Asking for a conversation with the wrong tenant looks up a different document name, which doesn't exist.
EmbeddingsOne Pinecone namespace per tenant (tenant-pyramid-yogshala). A search runs inside one namespace and physically cannot return another's data.
Facts, programmes, chunksKeyed by tenant id.
Who is askingThe widget sends a public site key. The server maps it to a tenant and checks the page's domain (the browser's Origin header) against that tenant's allowed list (Phase 1, section 9).
Cost and abusePer-visitor and per-tenant rate limits, a daily AI token budget per tenant, a daily cap on lead emails per tenant.
Analogy

A filter is a receptionist told "only give out files from the blue drawer to blue visitors". A namespace is giving each school its own locked room. The receptionist can have a bad day; the wall can't.

Say it in an interview

"Tenant isolation is structural rather than filter-based: Pinecone namespaces per tenant, compound document keys in Firestore, and every store method requires a tenant id. The site key is public by design; what protects it in browsers is the Origin check against the tenant's allowed domains, and what bounds abuse from scripts is rate limiting and a per-tenant daily token budget."

10. Key decisions and their trade-offs

Every real project is a list of choices, each with something given up. These are Sitechat's main ones, with the reasoning recorded at the time.

14. Challenges we faced

The product-level challenges. Technical ones are in the phase for the file involved.

1. Discovering what the product is for

What happened: the bot was built as a question-answerer. Watching real test conversations showed the valuable moment was the hand-off to a person, and that the bot often lost it: it would decide to contact the team, the visitor changed subject, and the request was never acted on until the visitor chased it twice (five minutes between intention and action). Change: reframed as lead capture; the server now holds "pending items" for each conversation and reminds the model each turn. Lesson: when something must happen every time, don't ask the model to remember; the server has the information.

2. Showing sources made the bot worse to use

What happened: replies listed their source passages as citations. The citation block was often longer than the answer. Change (15 Oct): the widget stopped showing sources, the API stopped returning them, and they're logged instead. A side effect appeared a day later: the model kept writing "[2]" markers it copied from older messages in conversations that began before the change, so the server now strips them.

3. Streaming felt robotic

What happened: word-by-word streaming (server-sent events) was built on 16 Oct. You found it felt robotic and it was reverted the same day. Change: reply bubbles: each paragraph appears as its own message with typing dots in between. Then the prompt was changed to write shorter paragraphs, because measurement showed 29 of 40 real replies would have been a single bubble.

4. The visitor CC as an abuse channel

See section 4: copying visitors on emails turned the product into a possible spam relay. Fixed with Reply-To, unclickable links and a daily cap.

5. Renaming a tenant is not a rename

What happened: the demo tenant was called demo-yoga, a bad name to show a real school. But the tenant id is part of every document id (demo-yoga__...) and of the Pinecone namespace, so there's no rename in place. Change: scripts/rename_tenant.py copies the record and visitor data under the new id (refusing if the new id already has data), then ingest, sync_facts and delete_tenant rebuild and clean up. Checked afterwards: 57 conversations, 27 leads, 95 vectors under the new id, nothing left under the old. Lesson: ids that are baked into storage keys are effectively permanent; choose them carefully.

6. Knowing when to stop

On 19 Oct a pitch kit, a course-picking fix, a reply-letter format and a slimmer deploy image were added, then all four reverted at your request. Reverting cleanly (one commit back to a known state) is a skill too: the history shows exactly what was tried and why it was undone.

Glossary

Tenant
One customer business and its data. Every piece of data carries a tenant id.
Site key
A public identifier in the script tag (pk_...) that tells the server which tenant a request is for. Not a secret.
Allowed domains
The websites a tenant's site key may be used from, checked against the browser's Origin header.
Lead
A record of one visitor's contact details and requests, kept separately from their conversation.
Lead capture
Getting interested visitors' contact details to the business. The product's real goal.
Pending item
Something the team still needs to act on for one conversation; today, a contact request.
Lead digest
The scheduled job that emails a visitor's questions once they've been quiet for 15 minutes.
Facts
Exact, changeable details (prices, dates) from the school's Google Sheet, sent to the model in full every time.
Documents / chunks
The school's descriptive text, split into sections ("chunks") so the relevant ones can be found per question.
Retrieval
Finding the chunks most relevant to a question (Phase 3).
Programme
One course or retreat a school offers. The bot tracks which one a visitor means ("focus").
Onboarding
Setting up a new tenant: record, documents, facts, evaluation, install.
Operator
Whoever runs the service (you), using the scripts.
Namespace
A separate partition inside a Pinecone index; one per tenant.
TTL (time to live)
Automatic deletion of a database document after a date stored on it.
Reply-To
An email header naming where replies should go, different from the sender.
Free tier
The usage a cloud service gives without charge each day or month.
Token budget
The most AI tokens a tenant's replies may use per day before the bot pauses until midnight UTC.

Check yourself

Why is "lead capture" a better description of Sitechat than "customer support", and name two features that only make sense under it.

The goal is that every interested visitor ends up known to the school, not just that questions get answered. Features: recording every question on the server for the email; the lead digest that emails even without a contact request; the always-visible "Talk to the team" button; labelling which questions weren't answered.

A school changes its 200-hour fee. What do they do, and why isn't the fee in the documents?

They edit their Google Sheet; it's re-read within a day (or at once with sync_facts). Prices live in facts because they must be exact and come from one place the school controls, and because retrieval by meaning can't reliably tell one course's fees section from another's.

Why can't a bug make school A's bot answer from school B's documents?

Each tenant's embeddings are in their own Pinecone namespace, and searches run inside one namespace. It's a structural wall, not a filter someone could forget.

Why is the visitor not copied on the lead email?

Anyone can type anyone's address. Copying would let a stranger make us send a victim an email containing the stranger's text: a spam and phishing relay. The visitor is the Reply-To instead, so the team's reply still reaches them.

Why does the suggested reply in the lead email come from a template and not the AI?

An AI draft would be tempted to fill in answers it doesn't know, and an invented answer sent under the school's name is the worst outcome. The template only thanks the visitor and leaves a gap under each open question.

What never reaches the AI model, and why?

Visitors' email addresses and phone numbers. They're removed and stored on the lead first, so they aren't re-sent with every later message or exposed in logs of the conversation.

Why is renaming a tenant a four-step process?

The tenant id is part of every document id and the Pinecone namespace. So data must be copied under the new id, documents and facts rebuilt, and the old data deleted.
